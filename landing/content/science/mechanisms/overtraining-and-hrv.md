## What does “overtraining” mean?

Training creates a useful challenge when the workload is balanced with enough recovery [S1]. A short-term drop in performance followed by improvement after recovery is called functional overreaching [S1, S4]. A longer performance decline without that rebound may be described as non-functional overreaching, while overtraining syndrome (OTS) involves a more prolonged loss of performance [S1, S4]. These terms describe different outcomes and should not be used as synonyms [S1, S4]. Distinguishing non-functional overreaching from OTS can be difficult and depends on the clinical course and the exclusion of other causes [S1].

## What can HRV show during training?

HRV is used in sport (see [exercise and HRV](/science/mechanisms/exercise-and-hrv)) as one signal that may add context to training status, adaptation and recovery [S5]. Repeated readings compared with an athlete's own usual pattern are more useful for personal monitoring than a single value judged against a population cut-off [S5]. The strength-and-conditioning review recommends keeping measurement conditions consistent, including the time of day, posture, resting state and device settings [S5]. HRV is best considered alongside performance, training load, perceived effort and other signs of recovery, not on its own [S1, S2, S5].

## Why do soccer and endurance studies differ?

A 2025 systematic review of adult soccer players found relationships between linear HRV measures and indicators such as performance, training load, fatigue and recovery in some included studies [S2]. The review rated the average study quality as fair and highlighted differences in HRV measures and definitions of overtraining [S2]. A separate 2021 systematic review of trained and professional endurance athletes found that changes in HRV did not consistently reflect functional overreaching [S3]. These reviews address different sports and different outcomes, so their findings should not be forced into one universal rule [S2, S3].

## Can HRV diagnose overtraining syndrome?

No: HRV cannot diagnose overtraining syndrome [S1, S4, S5]. The ECSS and ACSM consensus statement says that no set of markers has met all criteria for general acceptance [S1]. A scoping review describes OTS as a diagnosis of exclusion because there is no gold-standard test [S4]. It also reports an earlier analysis in which no individual marker accurately distinguished athletes with OTS from healthy athletes [S4]. HRV may be one part of a broader assessment, but it cannot establish an OTS diagnosis by itself [S1, S4, S5].

## Why can one HRV reading be misleading?

HRV changes with more than training [S5]. Psychological stress, acute illness, dehydration, alcohol or nicotine, travel and some medications can all affect the reading [S5]. See also [alcohol and HRV](/science/mechanisms/alcohol-and-hrv) and [sleep and HRV](/science/mechanisms/sleep-and-hrv). A changed value after a hard session may reflect the training stimulus as well as other influences, so the number alone cannot tell you whether recovery is inadequate [S1, S5]. This is one reason to avoid reacting to a small day-to-day change as if it were a diagnosis [S5].

## How should athletes use HRV trends in context?

A useful trend starts with repeated measurements taken in a similar way and compared with the same person's baseline [S5]. It can be reviewed alongside changes in performance, training load, perceived effort, mood, sleep and recovery [S1, S2, S5]. HRV alone should not trigger a major change in a training plan or be used to label an athlete as overtrained [S5]. Persistent performance decline or unexplained symptoms need a wider assessment rather than an HRV-only explanation [S1, S4]. If performance keeps falling despite rest, or you have symptoms such as chest pain, fainting, a racing heartbeat or ongoing exhaustion, see a doctor or sports medicine physician.

## What does the evidence show?

**Guideline and consensus evidence:** functional overreaching, non-functional overreaching and OTS need to be distinguished, and no single marker is accepted as a general diagnostic test [S1]. **Context-dependent monitoring evidence:** some soccer studies link HRV with training and recovery indicators, but methods and definitions vary [S2]. **Counter-evidence:** a systematic review of endurance athletes did not find HRV changes to be consistent markers of functional overreaching [S3]. **Diagnostic boundary:** OTS remains a diagnosis of exclusion, and HRV cannot diagnose it [S1, S4]. **Practical monitoring boundary:** repeated, standardized, within-person trends may be useful as one input, not as a stand-alone verdict [S5].

## What does HRV not tell you?

HRV does not provide a universal threshold that says an athlete is overtrained [S1, S4, S5]. It cannot tell you whether a performance decline is caused by functional overreaching, non-functional overreaching, OTS, illness, inadequate energy intake or another medical issue [S1, S4]. A wearable's readiness score or a low HRV value is not a clinical diagnosis [S4, S5]. The type of sport, the measurement method and the conditions around the recording all limit how far a result can be generalized [S2, S3, S5].
