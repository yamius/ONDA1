## How does shift work relate to HRV?

[Heart rate variability](/glossary/heart-rate-variability) (HRV) is the natural variation in time between heartbeats. Night and long shifts move sleep, meals and activity away from the body clock, and night work is linked with heart disease in large studies. Researchers have asked whether HRV shows part of that strain. In short: after a single long shift the findings are mixed, and in people who have worked nights for years the differences in classic HRV measures are small or absent [S1, S2, S4].

This page describes what studies have found. It does not give advice on shift schedules, light or melatonin, because there is no HRV evidence for them.

## How does it work?

The body clock times heart rate, blood pressure and the nervous system's activity across the day and night. Shift work asks the body to be awake and active when the clock expects rest, and to sleep when it expects activity. HRV measures such as [RMSSD](/science/concepts/rmssd) are read as reflecting vagally mediated changes in heart rate, so researchers expected night work to lower them.

The data only partly fit this idea. A review of long shifts found inconsistent results, with a suggested shift away from parasympathetic dominance [S1]. In adolescents, irregular and delayed sleep timing went with blunted HRV, while social jet lag, the gap between weekday and weekend sleep, did not [S5].

## How is it measured?

Studies differ a lot. Some record HRV right after a twenty-four-hour shift; others record a whole day off, or overnight sleep, months or years into shift work [S1, S2, S4]. Some compare night and day workers at one point in time, so they cannot show cause. Recording length, setting, breathing and analysis method all affect HRV values, which makes these studies hard to compare [S8].

One large study also measured heart rate fragmentation, an irregular switching pattern in heartbeats. It is a different measure from HRV and should not be read as one [S2].

## What affects it?

Sleep length and quality, sleep timing, meals, caffeine, alcohol, stress, fitness and age all affect HRV, and all of them change with shift work [S2, S5, S8]. That makes it hard to separate the effect of the shift itself from everything around it. Other everyday factors are explained in [Why is my HRV low?](/science/questions/why-is-my-hrv-low) and [Sleep and HRV](/science/mechanisms/sleep-and-hrv).

## What does the evidence show?

**After a long shift: context-dependent.** A systematic review of twenty-four-hour shifts found inconsistent effects on HRV, with only some studies showing a change and a suggested shift away from parasympathetic dominance [S1].

**Years of night work: context-dependent, mostly no difference.** In a large cohort with overnight ECG, classic HRV measures showed minimal differences between night, rotating and day workers; heart rate fragmentation was higher in night workers, and sleep length affected this [S2]. Workers with many years of night shifts showed no significant HRV differences from matched day workers [S4]. An older study found lower HRV during sleep in male shift workers only, but not overall and not in women, and this has not been repeated [S3].

**Meal timing: emerging.** In a small randomized trial of simulated night work, people who ate only in the daytime showed no significant change in vagal HRV markers, while those who ate at night and day showed a drop [S6]. This is one laboratory trial in healthy non-shift workers.

**Naps during shifts: context-dependent.** Medical night-shift workers who reported naps of about one to two hours during the shift had higher HRV on a day off [S7]. This is an association; it does not show that napping raises HRV.

**Jet lag: unknown.** There are no solid studies of HRV after flights across time zones. Social jet lag in adolescents was not linked with HRV [S5], and this cannot be carried over to travel.

## What it does not tell you

- **Not a measure of shift tolerance.** No HRV value shows whether someone copes well or badly with night work [S2, S4].
- **Not proof of harm or safety.** Mixed and null HRV findings do not mean shift work is harmless, and a lower reading does not mean harm [S1, S4].
- **Not about one night.** {{fact:claim.hrvNotStress}} [S8]. A low reading after a night shift may simply follow short or mistimed sleep.
- **Not a prescription.** The meal-timing and nap findings are early and are not a tested programme for shift workers [S6, S7].
