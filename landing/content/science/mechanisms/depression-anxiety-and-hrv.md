## What is the link between HRV, depression and anxiety?

[Heart rate variability](/glossary/heart-rate-variability) (HRV) is the natural variation in the time between heartbeats. Research has compared it between people with mental disorders and people without them for decades. The short version: on average, groups with some disorders show lower HRV, but the differences are modest, they depend on the disorder and the metric, and they do not work as a test for an individual [S1].

This page is about what those studies found. It is not about treating depression or anxiety, and it does not recommend any practice for them.

## How does it work?

HRV measures such as RMSSD and high-frequency power are read as reflections of vagally mediated changes in heart rate; {{fact:claim.vagalTone}} [S6]. The vagus nerve links the brainstem with the heart, and the same brain networks are involved in emotion and in regulating the heart. That is why researchers expected HRV and mood to be related.

The direction of the link is not known. Lower HRV may be part of a vulnerability, or it may follow the condition through poor sleep, less physical activity, other illnesses or medication. A meta-analysis of longitudinal studies describes HRV as reflecting mechanisms of vulnerability, with small effects [S3]. Treatment itself can change HRV: before-and-after studies of antipsychotic medication, brain stimulation, physiotherapy and psychotherapy found altered HRV, on weak evidence [S1]. So a person's HRV during treatment does not show the condition alone.

## How is it measured?

Most studies record HRV with an ECG in a laboratory, usually for a few minutes at rest, sometimes over a whole day. Depression and anxiety are measured separately, with interviews and questionnaires. The results depend on both choices. In PTSD, for example, the difference from controls was larger in short recordings than in all-day ones [S4]. Recording length, setting, breathing and analysis all shape the value, and guidelines ask for them to be reported and considered [S6].

Consumer watches and rings measure HRV differently, mostly at night and from the pulse at the skin. The research on mental disorders was not done with these values, and its findings do not transfer to them directly. How wearables measure HRV is covered in [can you trust HRV from a smartwatch or ring?](/science/measurements/heart-rate-variability)

## What affects it?

HRV is shaped by many things that also change with low mood or anxiety: sleep, physical activity, alcohol, illness, breathing during the recording, and medication [S1, S6]. This is one reason why studies disagree, and why a difference between groups cannot be pinned on the mental disorder itself. The everyday factors are covered in [why is my HRV low?](/science/questions/why-is-my-hrv-low)

## What does the evidence show?

**Mental disorders in general — context-dependent.** An umbrella review of meta-analyses (Wang et al., 2025) compared HRV between people with mental disorders and healthy controls across many conditions [S1]. On the authors' grading, the strongest evidence of lower HRV reached only a "suggestive" level, for dementia, PTSD, somatic symptom and functional somatic disorders, and schizophrenia [S1]. No disorder reached a higher level. No two disorders showed the same pattern, and the credibility of the findings varied by metric [S1].

**Depression and generalized anxiety — context-dependent, weaker.** For major depressive disorder and generalized anxiety disorder, the same review rated the evidence weaker, below the suggestive level [S1]. A difference may exist, but it is less certain than many popular articles suggest.

**Anxiety severity — context-dependent.** A meta-analysis of studies that measured both HRV and anxiety questionnaires found only a few significant associations, weak to moderate, and only for certain combinations of questionnaire and metric; most combinations showed none [S2]. One of them involved the LF/HF ratio, an index whose physiological meaning is disputed. The authors conclude that HRV is not a replacement for psychometric scales [S2].

**Later depressive symptoms — context-dependent.** A meta-analysis of longitudinal studies followed people who were not depressed at the start. Lower HRV was linked with more depressive symptoms later, but the correlations were small; the authors call the prospective effects small and context-dependent [S3]. That is far from a prediction for one person.

**PTSD — context-dependent.** A meta-analysis found vagally mediated HRV consistently lower in people with PTSD than in controls, with a larger difference in short recordings than in all-day ones [S4]. A critical review of the same field describes the differences as small, variable and context-dependent, and does not recommend HRV as a standalone clinical or screening tool [S5].

**Unknown.** Whether lower HRV comes before these conditions or follows them; whether consumer night-time HRV shows the same group differences; and whether HRV adds anything useful to a clinical assessment.

## What it does not tell you

- **Not a diagnosis.** No HRV value shows that someone has depression, anxiety or PTSD, and no HRV value rules them out [S2, S5].
- **Not a screening test or a forecast.** The prospective links are small, and HRV has not been shown to identify who will develop symptoms [S3].
- **Not your mood.** {{fact:claim.hrvNotStress}} [S6]. A low night can follow a short sleep, a drink, a cold or a hard workout.
- **Not a measure of treatment.** Medication and therapy can change HRV, so a change during treatment does not by itself say whether the treatment is working [S1].
- **When to get help.** If low mood, worry or anxiety last for weeks or get in the way of daily life, talk to a doctor or a mental health professional. If you have thoughts of harming yourself, contact emergency services or a crisis line now. Do not wait for a number on a device to confirm how you feel.
