## How is light related to HRV?

[Heart rate variability](/glossary/heart-rate-variability) (HRV) is the natural variation in time between heartbeats. Light reaching the eyes affects alertness, the body clock and sleep, so researchers have asked whether its brightness and color also change HRV. In short: in laboratory studies, bright blue light has generally gone with lower vagally mediated HRV and dim warm light with a possible rise, but every study so far has a high risk of bias [S1].

This page is about light seen by the eyes. Red and near-infrared light applied to the skin by devices is a different topic, covered in [Red light therapy](/science/evidence/red-light-therapy).

## How does it work?

Light, especially bright and blue-rich light, signals daytime to the brain and raises alertness. Researchers expected this arousal to shift the heart away from rest, lowering vagally mediated HRV, and dim warm light to do the opposite [S1]. Light also shapes the body clock and sleep, which affect HRV over longer periods; see [Sleep and HRV](/science/mechanisms/sleep-and-hrv).

## How is it measured?

Most studies expose people to light of a set brightness and color for minutes to hours in a laboratory and record HRV during or after exposure [S1]. Populations, exposure protocols and control conditions differ a lot between studies, which is one reason the findings are inconsistent [S1]. Recording length, setting, breathing and analysis also affect HRV values [S4].

## What affects it?

Time of day, sleepiness, breathing, posture, caffeine and stress all affect HRV and may interact with light [S4]. In real life, light comes together with activity, screens and work, so its own effect is hard to separate. Shift workers face light at unusual times; see [Shift work and HRV](/science/mechanisms/shift-work-and-hrv).

## What does the evidence show?

**Quality first: all studies at high risk of bias.** The only systematic review of light and vagally mediated HRV rated every included study as high risk of bias, and the authors say this limits clinical use [S1].

**Bright blue light: context-dependent.** Bright blue light was generally associated with lower vagally mediated HRV, likely because it promotes alertness [S1].

**Dim warm light: emerging.** Dim warm light, such as red or dim white light, showed potential to raise vagally mediated HRV, but findings were inconsistent [S1].

**Blue light with slow breathing: emerging, no HRV change.** In a workplace trial, slow breathing combined with blue light after a stress test lowered stress ratings and sleepiness and improved mood, but HRV did not change [S2]. Because breathing and light were combined, no effect can be credited to light alone.

**Light at night: no HRV data.** A scientific statement describes artificial light at night, together with air pollution and noise, as promoting mechanisms that raise cardiovascular risk [S3]. It has no data on HRV, and we found no pooled studies of light at night and HRV. Pollution and noise are covered in [Air pollution, noise and HRV](/science/mechanisms/air-pollution-noise-and-hrv).

**Unknown.** Whether regular light exposure changes HRV over weeks or months.

## What it does not tell you

- **Not a light therapy for HRV.** No long-term trials show that a lamp or filter raises HRV [S1].
- **Not proof for blue-light glasses.** These were not tested for HRV in the studies here.
- **Not about sizes.** With high risk of bias in all studies, only general directions can be described [S1].
- **Not about skin devices.** Red light therapy is a separate topic with its own evidence.
- **Not about one reading.** A reading taken in bright light or right after screen use may differ from one taken in the dark [S4].
