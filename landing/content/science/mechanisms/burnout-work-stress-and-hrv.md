## How are burnout and work stress related to HRV?

[Heart rate variability](/glossary/heart-rate-variability) (HRV) is the natural variation in time between heartbeats. Burnout is a state of exhaustion linked with long-term work stress. Because HRV changes during acute stress, many people expect it to show burnout too. In short: the links found so far are weak, and in one study they were largely explained by age and sex [S1]. There is no pooled analysis of HRV and burnout yet.

This page describes what studies have found. It does not recommend workplace programmes, and it is not a test for burnout.

## How does it work?

During acute stress, the nervous system shifts the heart towards readiness, and short-term HRV usually falls; this is described in [Acute stress and HRV](/science/mechanisms/acute-stress-and-hrv). The idea behind burnout research is that long-term stress might leave a lasting trace in resting HRV.

The evidence does not clearly show such a trace. In employees with people-facing jobs, HRV differed only slightly between burnout-risk groups, mostly over the whole day and not at night, and the effects were mainly driven by age and sex [S1]. In a ten-year cohort, work stress was not linked with resting HRV, while physical activity was [S5].

## How is it measured?

Burnout is measured with questionnaires. HRV is recorded over minutes, a working day, or a full twenty-four hours. Some studies compare people with higher and lower burnout scores; others compare stressful and calm periods within the same person's day [S1, S2]. These are different questions: a dip during a busy shift says nothing on its own about burnout. Recording length, setting, breathing and analysis all affect HRV values, which makes studies hard to compare [S6].

## What affects it?

Age and sex have large effects on HRV and can hide or create apparent links with burnout [S1]. Sleep, physical activity, alcohol, illness, medication, shift patterns and breathing also change HRV [S5, S6]. Everyday factors are explained in [Why is my HRV low?](/science/questions/why-is-my-hrv-low) and [Shift work and HRV](/science/mechanisms/shift-work-and-hrv).

## What does the evidence show?

**Burnout scores and HRV: context-dependent, weak.** In employees with people-facing jobs, correlations between HRV and burnout dimensions were mostly weak, and further analysis showed the effects were mainly driven by age and sex [S1].

**Stress and recovery during work: context-dependent.** A meta-analysis of doctors monitored through the day found HRV differed between stressful periods and recovery, but the studies were small, used different devices, and were of moderate quality at best [S2]. This compares moments within a day, not people with and without burnout.

**Workplace stress programmes: context-dependent, no advantage shown.** In a trial in one organisation, HRV biofeedback and mindfulness did not differ from each other or from a waiting-list group; all groups reported less stress over time [S3]. More on biofeedback in [HRV biofeedback](/science/evidence/hrv-biofeedback).

**App-based mindfulness: emerging, mixed.** In healthy adults, a short app-based mindfulness programme raised HRV during the sessions, but personal burnout scores rose right after the programme and only returned toward baseline later [S4].

**Work stress over ten years: context-dependent, no link.** In a large Belgian cohort, work stress was not associated with resting HRV ten years later [S5].

**Unknown.** Whether HRV can screen for or track burnout in one person; no study has tested this, and there is no pooled analysis.

## What it does not tell you

- **Not a burnout test.** No HRV value shows whether someone is burned out or not [S1, S2].
- **Not a measure of your workload.** A lower value may reflect age, sleep, illness or training as much as work [S1, S5].
- **Not about one day.** {{fact:claim.hrvNotStress}} [S6].
- **Not proof that a programme works.** Feeling better after a workplace programme can happen in a waiting-list group too [S3].
- **When to seek help.** If exhaustion, cynicism or low mood last for weeks or affect daily life, talk to a doctor or a mental health professional. If you have thoughts of harming yourself, contact emergency services or a crisis line straight away.
