## How are loneliness and social connections related to HRV?

[Heart rate variability](/glossary/heart-rate-variability) (HRV) is the natural variation in time between heartbeats. Loneliness and weak social ties are linked with poorer health in large studies, so researchers have asked whether the autonomic nervous system, and HRV as one of its markers, is part of that link. In short: the links found so far are small, appear only in some groups, and mostly fade once health and behaviour are taken into account [S1, S2].

This page covers what studies have found about loneliness, social support and time in nature. It does not recommend a number of friends, hours in a forest or any treatment.

## How does it work?

Measures such as RMSSD and high-frequency power are read as reflecting vagally mediated changes in heart rate; {{fact:claim.vagalTone}} [S10]. The idea behind the research is that feeling safe and supported may shape how the body responds to stress, while long-term isolation may keep stress systems more active.

The evidence points more to indirect paths. In older adults, the links between supportive ties and HRV vanished or shrank once physical health, behaviour and psychological factors were included [S1]. In a large cohort followed for ten years, physical activity tracked resting HRV, but social support did not [S2]. People with more support may move more, sleep better and have fewer illnesses, and each of these affects HRV on its own.

## How is it measured?

Most studies record HRV with an ECG for a few minutes at rest, or before, during and after a laboratory stress test. Loneliness and social support are measured with questionnaires. Some studies look at resting HRV; others look at how much HRV changes during a challenge, which is called reactivity. These are different questions: a weaker reaction under stress does not mean a low resting value [S3]. Recording length, setting, breathing and analysis all affect HRV values, and the guideline asks for them to be reported and considered [S10].

Nature studies usually compare a short forest or city walk, or a few minutes of nature sound against another sound. They measure changes over minutes or days, not over months.

## What affects it?

HRV depends on many things that also differ between people with more and fewer social ties: physical activity, sleep, age, illness, alcohol, medication and mood [S1, S2, S10]. That is why a link between social life and HRV in a group says little about cause. Everyday factors are explained in [Why is my HRV low?](/science/questions/why-is-my-hrv-low)

## What does the evidence show?

**Social ties: context-dependent.** In a German cohort of older adults, supportive friendship ties went with higher HRV in people who were unmarried or past retirement age, and family ties in people below retirement age [S1]. After adjusting for behaviour, physical and psychological health, these results vanished or were reduced [S1].

**Social support over ten years: emerging, no link found.** In a large Belgian cohort, more physical activity went with higher resting HRV ten years later, but social support, work stress and depressive symptoms did not [S2].

**Loneliness and stress reactivity: emerging.** In young adults who did a standard social stress test, those who felt lonelier showed a smaller HRV response [S3]. In another study of students, high resting HRV combined with a larger drop during a challenge went with fewer depressive symptoms and sleep problems in lonely participants [S4]. This is a statistical moderation, not a test of whether changing HRV helps.

**Forests: emerging for heart rhythm, context-dependent for mood.** Early field experiments across Japanese forests found lower pulse rate and more parasympathetic activity during forest walks than city walks [S5]. A meta-analysis of forest therapy trials found short-term reductions in self-reported stress, depression and anxiety, but with high heterogeneity, unblinded designs and self-report measures, and the authors call for cautious interpretation [S6]. In a small trial in older adults with hypertension, a three-day forest stay lowered blood pressure compared with a city stay [S7]. In a crossover trial in young adults, forest walks reduced perceived stress while city walks did not, but HRV was not measured [S8].

**Nature sounds: emerging.** In a short randomized crossover study, a nature soundscape raised HRV and lowered heart and breathing rate compared with a quiet café soundscape [S9]. The session lasted minutes in a laboratory, and slower breathing may explain part of the change.

**Unknown.** No study shows that reducing loneliness raises HRV, and there are no long-term studies of regular time in nature and HRV.

## What it does not tell you

- **Not a measure of social health.** No HRV value shows whether someone is lonely or well connected [S1, S2].
- **Not proof of cause.** The links are associations that mostly fade after adjustment for health and behaviour [S1].
- **Not about you on one night.** {{fact:claim.hrvNotStress}} [S10]. A low night after a lonely day may just as well follow poor sleep, alcohol or illness.
- **Not a treatment.** Forest stays and nature sounds have short-term effects in studies; they are not a treatment for hypertension, depression or anxiety [S6, S7].
- **When to seek help.** If loneliness or low mood lasts for weeks or affects daily life, talk to a doctor or a mental health professional. If you have thoughts of harming yourself, contact emergency services or a crisis line straight away.
