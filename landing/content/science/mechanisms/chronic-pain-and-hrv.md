## How is chronic pain related to HRV?

[Heart rate variability](/glossary/heart-rate-variability) (HRV) is the natural variation in time between heartbeats. Chronic pain involves the nervous system, not only the painful area, so researchers have compared HRV in people with and without long-lasting pain. In short: on average, people with chronic musculoskeletal pain, such as fibromyalgia or long-term back and neck pain, have lower resting HRV, mostly in vagal measures [S1].

This page describes what studies have found. It does not recommend any pain treatment and is not a way to measure pain.

## How does it work?

Measures such as RMSSD and high-frequency power are read as reflecting vagally mediated changes in heart rate. Pain, poor sleep, low activity, stress and medication can all shift how the nervous system controls the heart, and all of them are common in chronic pain. The lower HRV seen in groups with chronic pain may reflect any mix of these; studies comparing groups at one point in time cannot say which comes first [S1].

The pattern also differs by condition. In fibromyalgia, which affects the whole body, the differences were larger in longer recordings; in pain limited to one region, short recordings were enough to show them [S1].

## How is it measured?

Most studies record HRV at rest for a few minutes, some over a day, and compare people with pain with people without pain [S1]. Recording length, posture, breathing and analysis method all affect the values, and the guideline asks for them to be reported and considered [S4]. The pooled study concluded that recording protocols should fit the condition rather than follow one universal rule [S1].

## What affects it?

Sleep, physical activity, fitness, age, mood, alcohol and pain medication all affect HRV [S4]. Many of these change with chronic pain, which is why a lower group average says little about cause. Everyday factors are explained in [Why is my HRV low?](/science/questions/why-is-my-hrv-low) and [Sleep and HRV](/science/mechanisms/sleep-and-hrv).

## What does the evidence show?

**Resting HRV in chronic pain: established at group level.** A meta-analysis of studies comparing adults with chronic musculoskeletal pain and healthy controls found consistently lower HRV, most clearly in vagal measures, with substantial differences between studies [S1].

**Recording protocol: context-dependent.** In fibromyalgia, longer recordings showed larger vagal differences, while regional pain conditions were captured by shorter recordings [S1].

**Physiotherapy trials: context-dependent, no change shown.** Pooled trials of physiotherapy for low back and neck pain found no evidence that it changed autonomic measures compared with other treatments [S2]. The trials used many different treatments and comparisons and varied a lot, so this is not proof that the treatments have equal effects, and it says nothing about pain relief itself.

**Sensing the heartbeat: emerging, no link.** In in-patients with chronic pain, how accurately people sensed their heartbeat was not linked with their symptom reports [S3]. More on this in [Interoception](/science/concepts/interoception).

**Unknown.** Whether HRV can predict flare-ups or track pain in one person; there are no data on this.

## What it does not tell you

- **Not a pain meter.** No HRV value shows how much pain someone has [S1].
- **Not a diagnosis.** Lower average HRV in groups with chronic pain does not diagnose pain or its cause in one person [S1].
- **Not proof of cause.** The studies compare groups at one point in time and cannot show whether lower HRV comes before or after pain [S1].
- **Not about one night.** {{fact:claim.hrvNotStress}} [S4]. A low reading may follow a bad night or a flare-up as much as anything else.
- **When to seek help.** If pain lasts for months, worsens, or comes with weakness, numbness, fever or weight loss, see a doctor.
