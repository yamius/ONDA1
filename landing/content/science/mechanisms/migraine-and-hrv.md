## What is known about HRV in migraine?

Heart rate variability (HRV) is the beat-to-beat variation in heart rate [S2]. The main meta-analysis found no significant pooled HRV differences between people with migraine and control groups [S1]. The authors noted that differences in recording periods may have affected the results [S1]. Between-study heterogeneity was very high for some HRV measures, so those pooled estimates should not be treated as one typical migraine pattern [S1]. Current evidence does not establish a consistent resting-HRV signature for migraine [S1].

## Are resting HRV and autonomic reflex tests the same?

No. Resting HRV describes beat-to-beat variation during a recording, while autonomic reflex tests examine how the cardiovascular system responds to a specific challenge [S2]. These tests include deep breathing, standing, a sustained handgrip and the Valsalva manoeuvre [S2]. A deep-breathing test measures the heart's response to the breathing challenge, rather than a routine resting smartwatch score [S2].

A meta-analysis found lower results on several standardised autonomic tests in migraine groups during headache-free periods [S2]. This is evidence of group-level differences in autonomic responses, not proof that resting HRV is low in every person with migraine [S1, S2]. The authors also noted important risk-of-bias concerns and limits in the timing of the measurements [S2].

## Can a wearable predict an attack?

A 2026 systematic review examined wearable-derived and ambulatory signals used to forecast migraine or headache [S3]. No study in that review reported external validation, and calibration was rarely reported [S3]. The authors concluded that these models remain early proof-of-concept research, not clinically validated alert systems [S3]. They also warned that a model may appear to perform well because some people have more headache days than others, rather than because it can forecast an attack within one person [S3].

That distinction matters. Recognising a person who often has headaches is not the same as predicting when that person will have the next attack [S3]. The review also found no evidence that acting on wearable forecasts improves clinical outcomes [S3].

## What do newer wearable studies actually measure?

A wrist-biosensor study measured electrodermal activity, heart rate, skin temperature and activity-related signals while testing migraine prediction models [S4]. Although the device stream included HRV, the researchers excluded HRV from the prediction features because daytime ambulatory HRV was considered insufficiently reliable [S4]. Personalised models performed better than generalised models, but the authors called for larger prospective validation and real-time testing before routine clinical use [S4].

A separate nocturnal pilot combined pulse-rate variability (PRV), electrodermal activity, respiratory rate and sleep measures [S5]. PRV is derived from a pulse signal and is not identical to ECG-derived HRV [S5]. Group averages did not significantly distinguish nights before headache days from headache-free nights, and individual model performance varied considerably [S5]. These studies test combinations of signals; they do not show that HRV alone can predict migraine [S4, S5].

## What do camera-based studies add?

A camera-only feasibility preprint reported a group-level rise in heart rate before attacks, but other signals did not remain different after statistical correction [S6]. Individual prediction was weak in this small study [S6]. Because the report is a preprint and has not been peer reviewed, it should be treated as an early research lead, not evidence of a usable phone-camera migraine alert [S6].

## What does the evidence show?

**Context-dependent — resting HRV.** The available meta-analysis did not find a consistent pooled difference in resting HRV between migraine and control groups, and some measures had very high heterogeneity [S1].

**Context-dependent — autonomic reflex tests.** Standardised tests found group-level differences during headache-free periods, but the included studies had risk-of-bias and timing limitations [S2].

**Emerging — wearable prediction.** Wearable and camera-based models show proof of concept, but they lack the validation needed for dependable individual forecasts [S3, S4, S5, S6].

**Unknown — clinical benefit.** It is not established whether acting on a wearable migraine forecast improves health outcomes [S3].

## What does it not tell you?

HRV does not diagnose migraine, identify its subtype or reliably predict an individual attack [S1, S3, S8]. A normal reading does not rule out migraine, and an unusual reading does not prove that a migraine is starting [S1, S3]. PRV from a wrist sensor, ECG-derived HRV and standard autonomic reflex tests are different measurements and should not be treated as interchangeable [S2, S4, S5].

Seek urgent medical care for a sudden 'worst-ever' headache, or a headache with weakness, confusion, vision loss or fever [S7].
