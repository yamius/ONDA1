## What is being measured when studies say “smoking”?

For a practical overview of nicotine, vaping and heart rate, see [nicotine, vaping and HRV](/articles/nicotine-vaping-hrv-heart-rate). The studies on this page do not measure one identical exposure [S1, S2, S3, S4, S5]. Some examine combustible tobacco use and smoking history, some study nicotine given on its own, and one compares tobacco smokers with e-cigarette users and non-smokers [S1, S3, S4, S5]. The large wearable study paired daily self-reported tobacco use with overnight measurements; it did not identify tobacco use from a heart signal [S2]. These exposure types should not be treated as interchangeable [S1, S2, S3, S4, S5].

## What happens to HRV after acute nicotine exposure?

Two small randomized studies in healthy nonsmokers found that acute oral nicotine exposure changed short-term HRV measures [S4, S5]. The studies used controlled, short-term exposure, so they do not show what happens during years of smoking or vaping [S4, S5]. Their results also do not establish that every HRV measure changes in the same way [S5]. An acute nicotine challenge is not the same question as the long-term effect of combustible tobacco smoke [S1, S4, S5].

## What do population studies show about habitual smoking?

The CHRIS study examined a large group of adults and measured HRV from a short resting ECG [S1]. It found that heavier current smoking and greater cumulative exposure were associated with lower HRV among current smokers [S1]. The authors also noted that earlier population-based studies had reported conflicting results and that a simple current/former/never label can hide differences in smoking intensity [S1]. Because the study was cross-sectional, it cannot show that smoking caused a change in HRV or predict what will happen to one person's readings [S1].

## What do wearable studies add?

A longitudinal observational study followed a large group of WHOOP members using daily self-reported tobacco-use entries and wearable-derived overnight measures [S2]. Within individuals, nights after reported tobacco-use days were associated with lower overnight HRV than nights after reported non-use days [S2]. This is a real-world association, not proof that the wearable can detect tobacco use from HRV or that tobacco caused every change in the reading [S2]. The study used one commercial wearable system, was funded by WHOOP, and several authors were company employees with stock options [S2].

## What happens to HRV after someone reduces or stops tobacco use?

The evidence does not show one clear HRV recovery pattern after stopping or reducing tobacco use [S1, S2]. In the CHRIS study, past smokers had higher HRV than never-smokers, but the study did not follow the same people before and after quitting [S1]. In the WHOOP study, HRV did not significantly improve among participants who reduced tobacco use, even though some other measures changed in a more favourable direction [S2]. These results cannot establish how HRV changes within one person after quitting or how long any change may last [S1, S2].

## Why do studies sometimes disagree?

Studies use different definitions of smoking, different amounts of exposure and different periods without tobacco before measurement [S1, S3, S4, S5]. They also use different methods, including short resting ECG recordings, acute laboratory challenges and overnight data from a commercial wearable [S1, S2, S4, S5]. A small case-control study found no significant resting HRV differences between tobacco smokers, e-cigarette users and non-smokers after abstinence before testing [S3]. Its authors note that the study was exploratory and too small to rule out smaller differences [S3]. A null result in one small sample does not erase the other findings, but it does show why simple universal claims are not justified [S1, S2, S3].

## Can HRV detect tobacco use or verify quitting?

No study in this evidence set validates HRV as a way to identify tobacco use, nicotine exposure or dependence [S1, S2, S3, S4, S5]. The wearable cohort relied on self-reported tobacco use rather than detecting it from physiological signals [S2]. HRV also cannot confirm that someone has stopped smoking or show whether any recovery is complete [S1, S2]. A high or low HRV reading should not be used as a test of smoking status [S1, S2, S3].

## What does the evidence support?

**Acute exposure:** small randomized studies show that nicotine can change short-term HRV measures in healthy nonsmokers [S4, S5]. **Habitual smoking:** a large cross-sectional population study links smoking intensity and cumulative exposure with lower HRV among current smokers, while a small recent case-control study found no significant resting differences between groups [S1, S3]. **Wearable trends:** one large commercial-platform study found lower nocturnal HRV on self-reported tobacco-use days, but it did not validate tobacco detection [S2]. **After reduction or quitting:** results do not establish a reliable HRV recovery pattern; the WHOOP analysis did not find a significant HRV improvement among people who reduced use [S1, S2]. Overall, HRV is a context-sensitive physiological measure, not a smoking detector or cessation test [S1, S2, S3, S4, S5].

## In ONDA, what does the app do?

ONDA builds a personal baseline from HRV, resting heart rate and respiratory rate values in Apple Health [S6]. ONDA does not identify tobacco use, measure nicotine exposure or verify smoking cessation. It is not a smoking-cessation tool.

Whatever your HRV shows, stopping smoking is recommended for your health. A doctor, pharmacist or local stop-smoking service can help you plan quitting and choose support that works for you.
