## What is the link between the environment and HRV?

[Heart rate variability](/glossary/heart-rate-variability) (HRV) changes with what happens around us, not only with what we do. Environmental researchers use it as one of the early signals of how air pollution, heat and noise act on the heart and blood vessels. The research is about groups of people and small average shifts. It is not about what a single reading on a watch means.

## How does it work?

Fine particles in polluted air (particles small enough to reach deep into the lungs) are thought to affect the heart partly through the nervous system: irritation in the lungs and inflammation may shift the regulation of heart rate. Researchers treat lower HRV as one possible pathway by which air pollution raises cardiovascular risk, while the exact mechanism is still being studied [S1]. A scientific statement on pollution and heart disease describes air pollution, chronic noise and light at night as acting through damage to blood-vessel walls, oxidative stress, inflammation and blood clotting [S4]. Heat adds its own load: the body works to shed warmth, and heart rate rises.

## How is it measured?

Most pollution studies are panel studies: the same people are measured repeatedly, and their HRV is compared on days with cleaner and dirtier air. The heat study used personal temperature sensors and wearable HRV sensors on workers during their working days [S3]. Respirator studies randomly assign people to wear a particle-filtering mask or not, for hours to a couple of days [S2]. How HRV is recorded — the device, the length of the recording, the setting and breathing — changes the values, so results from different studies are not directly comparable [S5].

## What affects it?

Outdoor exposure varies with traffic, season, weather and indoor air. HRV itself varies with sleep, alcohol, illness, training and breathing, usually much more visibly than with air quality from one day to the next. The everyday factors are covered in [why is my HRV low?](/science/questions/why-is-my-hrv-low) and [why HRV changes from day to day](/science/mechanisms/hrv-day-to-day).

## What does the evidence show?

**Air pollution — context-dependent.** A meta-analysis of panel studies from North America, Asia and Europe (Niu et al., 2020) found that short-term exposure to fine particles was associated with lower HRV, across SDNN, RMSSD and high- and low-frequency power [S1]. The changes per step of pollution were small percentages. In exploratory subgroups the link was stronger in Asian populations, in healthy people and in people aged forty and over [S1]. The authors note that earlier results had been inconsistent and that the mechanism still needs clarifying [S1].

**Respirators — context-dependent, modest.** A meta-analysis of randomized trials (Faridi et al., 2022) compared wearing particle-filtering respirators with not wearing them, for hours to a couple of days [S2]. Blood pressure did not change significantly. High-frequency HRV power rose, and the LF/HF ratio fell slightly, while other HRV measures did not change [S2]. The authors call the improvements modest or non-significant and note that the trials were few and varied [S2]. This is not a recommendation to wear a respirator; it shows how little short-term protection changes the measured signal.

**Heat at work — emerging.** In a small panel of farm workers in orchards, rice fields and greenhouses (Lung et al., 2026), hotter working conditions were associated with lower HRV indicators and a higher heart rate [S3]. It is one small study of heavy occupational heat, not a finding about a warm day at home. Heat for wellness, such as sauna, is a different question, covered in [sauna and heat exposure](/science/evidence/sauna-heat-exposure).

**Noise — unknown for HRV.** A scientific statement on pollution and cardiovascular disease counts chronic noise among the environmental risk factors for the heart and blood vessels [S4]. We did not find a systematic review of noise and HRV, so we cannot say how large any HRV effect is. The absence of a review is a gap in the research, not evidence that noise has no effect.

**The bigger picture — guideline.** The same statement describes air pollution as the most significant environmental determinant of cardiovascular health worldwide [S4]. That is a population-health conclusion. The HRV findings are one small piece of it.

## What it does not tell you

- **Not an air-quality meter.** The pooled changes are small population averages; a low reading on a smoggy day cannot be attributed to the air [S1, S5].
- **Not a personal risk forecast.** Population links between pollution and heart disease do not turn one person's HRV into a risk estimate [S4, S5].
- **Not a verdict on a mask.** Respirator trials were short and small, and most HRV measures did not change [S2].
- **Not your stress level.** {{fact:claim.hrvNotStress}} [S5].
- **When to see a doctor.** Seek urgent care for chest pain, fainting, severe breathlessness, or palpitations with dizziness, whatever the air quality. If heat makes you confused, dizzy or stops you sweating, get out of the heat and seek help.
