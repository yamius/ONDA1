## What happens to HRV in pregnancy?

Pregnancy changes the heart and circulation: blood volume grows and resting heart rate usually rises. [Heart rate variability](/glossary/heart-rate-variability) (HRV), the natural variation in time between heartbeats, changes too. In short: in most studies HRV falls as pregnancy progresses, and it is lower still in preeclampsia, but these are directions from small, varied studies, and HRV does not predict how a pregnancy will go [S1, S2, S4].

This page explains what studies have found. It is not medical advice and does not replace prenatal care.

## How does it work?

The [autonomic nervous system](/science/concepts/autonomic-nervous-system) helps the body adapt to pregnancy. In a systematic review of healthy pregnancies, most studies found that time-domain and most frequency-domain HRV measures fell from the first to the third trimester, while the ratio of low to high frequency power tended to rise, which the authors read as more sympathetic activity late in pregnancy [S1]. That ratio is not a clean measure of the sympathetic system, so this reading is an interpretation.

## How is it measured?

Most studies record an ECG, from a few minutes to a full day, and report several HRV measures [S1]. The studies did not explain why they chose their tools, timing or methods, which limits how far the patterns can be generalized [S1]. Recording length, setting, breathing and analysis all affect HRV values [S5].

## What affects it?

The stage of pregnancy matters, so a value in late pregnancy cannot be compared with one from before pregnancy [S1]. Blood pressure disorders matter too: in preeclampsia, overall HRV was lower than in pregnancies with normal blood pressure [S2]. Sleep, stress and activity change during pregnancy as well, so a single reading has many possible causes. How HRV changes across the menstrual cycle is covered in [Menstrual cycle and HRV](/science/mechanisms/menstrual-cycle-and-hrv).

## What does the evidence show?

**Healthy pregnancy: emerging.** In most studies, HRV measures fell from the first to the third trimester [S1]. The studies were few, small and used different methods.

**Preeclampsia and high blood pressure in pregnancy: emerging.** A systematic review found lower overall HRV in preeclampsia than in pregnancies with normal blood pressure [S2]. The authors conclude that these disorders involve sympathetic overdrive with parasympathetic withdrawal, but the studies differed widely in methods and results [S2].

**Other complications: emerging.** Another review suggests autonomic function is linked with some common complications, including fetal growth [S3]. Existing studies do not support a link between autonomic markers and gestational diabetes [S3].

**Pregnancy outcomes: unknown.** A large review of variability in pregnancy found no study that reported HRV together with pregnancy outcomes [S4]. No data is not the same as no link, but it means HRV cannot be used to predict outcomes.

## What it does not tell you

- **Nothing about pregnancy outcomes.** No study in a large review linked HRV to outcomes [S4].
- **Not a screening test.** Lower HRV in preeclampsia is a group difference, not a way to detect it in one person [S2].
- **No norms for pregnancy.** Methods differ too much to set values or thresholds [S1, S2].
- **No evidence that raising HRV helps.** There are no trials showing that changing HRV improves pregnancy outcomes.
- **When to get help.** High blood pressure, sudden swelling of the face or hands, a severe headache, vision changes or pain under the ribs need a doctor or midwife right away, whatever an app shows.
