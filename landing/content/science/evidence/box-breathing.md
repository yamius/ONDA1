## What is box breathing?

Box breathing, also called square breathing, has four equal phases: breathe in, hold, breathe out, hold, each for the same count. It is widely promoted for staying calm under pressure. Researchers have tested whether it lowers stress responses and changes [heart rate variability](/glossary/heart-rate-variability) (HRV), the natural variation in time between heartbeats. In short: it lowers some acute stress responses, but it has not been shown to change HRV, and the evidence base is small [S1].

This page describes what trials have found. It is not a medical treatment.

## How does it work?

Box breathing slows the breath and adds pauses. Slower breathing lets heart rate rise and fall more with each breath, which is why slow breathing usually raises vagally mediated HRV during practice; this is covered in [Breathing and HRV](/science/mechanisms/breathing-and-hrv). The holds in box breathing make the cycle less smooth than simple slow breathing, and after hard exercise they may feel like extra effort [S4].

## How is it measured?

Trials compare a few minutes of box breathing with normal breathing or another technique, usually around a stress test or after exercise. They measure heart rate, HRV, saliva markers such as alpha-amylase and cortisol, and questionnaires on anxiety [S1, S2]. Most outcomes are measured within minutes, not weeks. Recording length, setting, breathing pace and analysis all affect HRV values [S6].

## What affects it?

The comparison matters most. Against unstructured breathing, box breathing can look helpful; against breathing at a slow steady pace, it can look worse [S3, S4]. The setting matters too: a calm laboratory, a social stress test, recovery after maximal effort or a real stressful moment all give different results [S2, S3, S5]. More comparisons are in [Breathing techniques compared](/science/mechanisms/breathing-techniques-compared).

## What does the evidence show?

**Acute stress responses: context-dependent.** A systematic review of randomized trials concluded that box breathing reduces some acute stress responses, such as heart rate, state anxiety and salivary alpha-amylase, but not HRV or cortisol [S1]. Benefits for perceived stress, anxiety and mood were inconsistent across trials [S1].

**Before a social stress test: context-dependent.** Box breathing and prolonged exhalation both buffered rises in heart rate, anxiety and alpha-amylase compared with normal breathing, and neither changed HRV, cortisol or cognitive performance [S2]. Box breathing did no better than simply breathing out slowly.

**After exercise: debated.** In cadets after a maximal test, box breathing and cyclic sighing gave larger rises in high-frequency HRV than spontaneous breathing [S3]. In students after interval training, box breathing led to higher heart rate and perceived exertion than breathing at six breaths per minute [S4]. The settings and comparisons differ, so these results cannot be merged into one verdict.

**In daily life: emerging.** In a pilot study, one minute of box breathing during stressful moments lowered anxiety more than a control condition, but reaction times were longer afterwards [S5].

**Unknown.** Long-term effects of regular box breathing, and whether it works better than other slow breathing techniques.

## What it does not tell you

- **Not an HRV booster.** The trials did not find HRV changes from box breathing under stress [S1, S2].
- **Not better than simpler slow breathing.** Where compared, slow exhalation worked as well, and slow steady breathing did better after interval training [S2, S4].
- **Not long-term evidence.** The outcomes are measured over minutes [S1].
- **Not free of trade-offs.** Reaction times were slower right after one minute of practice in daily life [S5].
- **Not about one reading.** A single HRV value after practice reflects that moment and your breathing at the time [S6].
