## What are tai chi and qigong?

Tai chi and qigong are traditional Chinese mind-body practices that combine slow movement, posture, attention and calm breathing. The family also includes Baduanjin, Wuqinxi, Yijinjing and Liuzijue, a breathing practice with soft vocal sounds. Researchers have asked whether these practices change [heart rate variability](/glossary/heart-rate-variability) (HRV), the natural variation in time between heartbeats, and whether they help with conditions such as high blood pressure.

This page describes what studies have found. It is not a training guide and does not replace medical care.

## How does it work?

Tai chi and qigong are gentle exercise done with slow, deep breathing. Both slower breathing and regular light exercise are linked with higher vagally mediated HRV, so these practices may act through familiar routes rather than anything unique; the studies do not separate them. Measures such as [RMSSD](/science/concepts/rmssd) are read as reflecting vagally mediated changes in heart rate; {{fact:claim.vagalTone}} [S5].

## How is it measured?

Most trials record resting HRV before and after a course of practice lasting weeks, and compare it with a control group. The meta-analysis pooled only time-domain measures, [SDNN](/science/concepts/sdnn) and RMSSD [S1]. Other trials measured blood pressure, blood markers or walking distance instead of HRV [S3, S4]. Recording length, setting, breathing and analysis all affect HRV values [S5].

## What affects it?

The starting point matters: people whose autonomic function was preserved at the start showed larger HRV gains [S1]. Breathing pace during practice, fitness, age, sleep and health also shape the results [S5]. More on exercise in [Exercise and HRV](/science/mechanisms/exercise-and-hrv) and on breathing in [Slow breathing](/science/evidence/slow-breathing).

## What does the evidence show?

**Tai chi and qigong and HRV: context-dependent.** A meta-analysis of studies in adults new to the practice found moderate short-term improvements in SDNN and RMSSD compared with control conditions [S1]. The studies were mostly small and had some concerns about bias. Gains were larger when starting autonomic function was preserved, and simpler or more complex forms worked similarly [S1].

**Liuzijue breathing: emerging.** In a small crossover trial, five minutes of Liuzijue breathing before mindful breathing gave more sustained relaxation, larger heart rate falls and greater HRV rises than mindful breathing alone [S2]. The participants were male students with anxiety symptoms, and breathing was slower during Liuzijue.

**Blood pressure: emerging, not about HRV.** A network meta-analysis of trials in middle-aged and older adults with high blood pressure ranked tai chi highest for lowering blood pressure among traditional Chinese exercises [S3]. The ranking is a probability, many trials came from Chinese databases, and HRV was not measured.

**Heart failure rehabilitation: context-dependent, not about HRV.** Added to standard rehabilitation, traditional Chinese exercises improved heart failure markers, walking distance and quality of life compared with conventional treatment alone [S4]. These are add-on results, not a replacement for medical care.

**Unknown.** Whether HRV changes last beyond the course, and whether experienced practitioners show the same effects.

## What it does not tell you

- **Not a long-term effect.** The HRV results are short-term and come from beginners [S1].
- **Not proof of a special mechanism.** Slow breathing and gentle exercise may explain the HRV changes [S1, S2].
- **Not HRV evidence from blood pressure or heart failure trials.** Those trials measured other outcomes [S3, S4].
- **Not a treatment.** Tai chi is not a replacement for blood pressure medication or cardiac rehabilitation [S3, S4].
- **Not about one session.** A single reading after practice reflects that moment, not a lasting change [S5].
