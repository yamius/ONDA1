## What is yoga, and why study it with HRV?

Yoga combines postures, breathing practices called pranayama, relaxation and meditation. Studies use very different mixes, so "yoga" is not one intervention. Researchers have asked whether yoga changes [heart rate variability](/glossary/heart-rate-variability) (HRV), the natural variation in time between heartbeats, and whether it helps with anxiety. In short: small studies show positive effects, mostly against waiting lists or no practice, and the best form and dose are unknown [S1, S2].

This page covers yoga and yoga breathing. Guided deep relaxation is covered separately in [Yoga nidra and NSDR](/science/evidence/yoga-nidra-nsdr).

## How does it work?

Breathing pace is the clearest link. Slow breathing lets heart rate rise and fall more with each breath, which raises vagally mediated HRV during practice; fast breathing does the opposite. One crossover trial showed this within yoga itself: slow humming breath and bellows breath raised a vagal HRV measure, while fast breathing lowered it [S4]. Slow breathing in general is covered in [Slow breathing](/science/evidence/slow-breathing), and fast techniques in [Fast breathing](/science/evidence/fast-breathing).

## How is it measured?

Trials compare a course of yoga lasting weeks, or a single session, with a waiting list, no practice, a neutral activity or standard care. They measure resting HRV, anxiety or symptom questionnaires and sometimes reflex tests [S1, S2, S3]. Measurements during practice reflect breathing at that moment, not a lasting change. Recording length, setting, breathing and analysis all affect HRV values [S6].

## What affects it?

The comparison group matters most: yoga looks better against waiting lists than against active care [S2]. The type of breathing matters too, slow or fast [S4]. Experience, the population studied and whether yoga is added to other treatment also shape the results [S3, S4].

## What does the evidence show?

**Students: context-dependent.** A meta-analysis of four trials in students found positive effects of yoga on anxiety and HRV, and the authors call for more rigorous studies to find the best form of practice [S1].

**Mental disorders: context-dependent.** In people with PTSD, depression and other non-psychotic disorders, pranayama reduced symptoms compared with passive controls, while results were similar to standard care such as psychotherapy or antidepressants [S2]. Four of six trials had a high risk of bias. Side effects were reported more often with fast than with slow breathing [S2].

**Anxiety disorder and HRV: emerging.** In one trial, eight weeks of slow pranayama and relaxation added to routine psychiatric care shifted HRV towards parasympathetic measures, compared with routine care alone [S3]. The control group did no breathing practice.

**Single sessions: emerging.** In experienced practitioners, slow humming and bellows yoga breathing raised a vagal HRV measure during practice, while fast yoga breathing lowered it [S4]. In people with migraine, one session of yoga-based breathing biofeedback improved spatial working memory and heartbeat-counting accuracy compared with a documentary, though one memory score rose in both groups [S5].

**Unknown.** Which form, dose and length of yoga works best, and whether effects last.

## What it does not tell you

- **Not a treatment for anxiety on its own.** Benefits were shown mainly against passive controls, not as a replacement for care [S2].
- **Not one practice.** Results from one yoga programme do not transfer to another [S1].
- **Not proof that fast breathing helps HRV.** Fast yoga breathing lowered vagal HRV during practice and was linked with more side effects [S2, S4].
- **Not about a reading during practice.** HRV measured while breathing slowly reflects that breathing, not your resting state [S6].
- **When to seek help.** If anxiety or low mood lasts for weeks or affects daily life, talk to a doctor or mental health professional.
